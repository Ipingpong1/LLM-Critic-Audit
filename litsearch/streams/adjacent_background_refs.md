# Human signal-detection background (adjacent stream)

No full texts were read for this section.

Marked **NOT VERIFIED / PARTLY VERIFIED** at the end: items Crossref doesn't hold (the Green & Swets 1966 and Egan 1975 books, the original Edwards 1968 chapter) or whose attribution I couldn't confirm ("sluggish beta").

One point matters throughout for the pilot. Almost every human base-rate and payoff study below teaches the base rate or payoff through **experience and trial-by-trial feedback**. The pilot instead **states** the base rate once, with no feedback. Where human studies removed feedback or relied on description, criterion shifts were weak or absent (Estes & Maddox 1995; Rhodes & Jacoby 2007). Compare the pilot's ρ with the human literature only with that caveat.

## A. Ideal observer and SDT foundations

- Tanner, W. P., & Swets, J. A. (1954). A decision-making theory of visual detection. *Psychological Review*, 61(6), 401–409. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0058700. Crossref: verified. [title] The founding statement of SDT for human vision: detection treated as a statistical decision with an adjustable criterion.
- Swets, J. A., Tanner, W. P., & Birdsall, T. G. (1961). Decision processes in perception. *Psychological Review*, 68(5), 301–340. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0040547. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Recasts statistical decision theory as a theory of signal detection that specifies an ideal process, and applies it to human observers.
- Swets, J. A. (1986). Indices of discrimination or diagnostic accuracy: Their ROCs and implied models. *Psychological Bulletin*, 99(1), 100–117. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.99.1.100. Crossref: verified. [title] Compares discrimination indices by the ROC shapes they imply; a standard source for why ROC-based (not single-point) indices are preferred.
- Geisler, W. S. (2011). Contributions of ideal observer theory to vision research. *Vision Research*, 51(7), 771–781. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.visres.2010.09.027. Crossref: verified. [title] Review of ideal-observer analysis as a benchmark for human performance.
- Maloney, L. T., & Zhang, H. (2010). Decision-theoretic models of visual perception and action. *Vision Research*, 50(23), 2362–2374. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.visres.2010.09.031. Crossref: verified. [title] Review of Bayesian decision theory (priors, likelihoods, gain functions) as a model of perception and action.
- Whiteley, L., & Sahani, M. (2008). Implicit knowledge of visual uncertainty guides decisions with asymmetric outcomes. *Journal of Vision*, 8(3), 2. https://doi.org/10.1167/8.3.2. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Under an externally imposed asymmetric loss in a simple visual choice task, observers approach the Bayes-optimal strategy. This is a counterexample to universal payoff conservatism.
- Lynn, S. K., & Barrett, L. F. (2014). "Utilizing" signal detection theory. *Psychological Science*, 25(9), 1663–1673. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614541991. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Tutorial: adding economic utility turns SDT into a model of optimal decisions under base rates and costs, not just an analysis method.
- Lynn, S. K., Wormwood, J. B., Barrett, L. F., & Quigley, K. S. (2015). Decision making from economic and signal detection perspectives: Development of an integrated framework. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 6, 952. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00952. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Integrates outcome-value uncertainty (economics) with perceptual uncertainty (SDT) in one decision framework.
- Rahnev, D., & Denison, R. N. (2018). Suboptimality in perceptual decision making. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 41, e223. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X18000936. Crossref: verified (no page or article number in the record). [abs-OA] Reviews eight classes of suboptimal perceptual decisions, including improper criterion placement under unequal priors and rewards. The best single entry point for "humans are conservative too."

## B. Criterion placement under base rates and payoffs: conservatism ("sluggish beta")

- Lee, W. (1963). Choosing among confusably distributed stimuli with specified likelihood ratios. *Perceptual and Motor Skills*, 16(2), 445–467. https://doi.org/10.2466/pms.1963.16.2.445. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Choices with overlapping distributions fell between statistical decision theory and a probability "micromatching" model, in which responses follow the likelihood ratio probabilistically.
- Lee, W., & Janke, M. (1964). Categorizing externally distributed stimulus samples for three continua. *Journal of Experimental Psychology*, 68(4), 376–382. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0042770. Crossref: verified. [title] An early study of cutoff placement with externally distributed stimuli; often cited for non-optimal cutoffs.
- Ulehla, Z. J. (1966). Optimality of perceptual decision criteria. *Journal of Experimental Psychology*, 71(4), 564–569. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0023007. Crossref: verified. [secondary] Cited by Bohil & Maddox (2001) and Rahnev & Denison (2018) among classic demonstrations that criteria shift in the optimal direction but not far enough (conservative cutoff placement).
- Kubovy, M., Rapoport, A., & Tversky, A. (1971). Deterministic vs probabilistic strategies in detection. *Perception & Psychophysics*, 9(5), 427–429. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03210245. Crossref: verified by bibliographic query. (My first DOI guess, 10.3758/BF03210244, resolves to a different paper, Wood 1971.) [title] Asks whether observers use a fixed cutoff or respond probabilistically, which bears on whether "conservatism" is a misplaced cutoff or probability matching.
- Dusoir, A. E. (1975). Treatments of bias in detection and recognition models: A review. *Perception & Psychophysics*, 17(2), 167–178. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03203882. Crossref: verified. [title] A review of how bias is modelled and how it responds to priors and payoffs.
- Kubovy, M. (1977). A possible basis for conservatism in signal detection and probabilistic categorization tasks. *Perception & Psychophysics*, 22(3), 277–281. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03199690. Crossref: verified. [title] Proposes an explanation for conservative cutoffs. Reply: Dusoir (1978), *Perception & Psychophysics*, 23(6), 542–545, https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03199532 (found in the same Crossref query; [title]).
- Kubovy, M., & Healy, A. F. (1977). The decision rule in probabilistic categorization: What it is and how it is learned. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 106(4), 427–446. https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.106.4.427. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Numerical analogue of signal detection (heights from two normal distributions 1 SD apart). Compares free responding with a forced reported cutoff to examine the decision rule and how it is learned.
- Healy, A. F., & Kubovy, M. (1977). A comparison of recognition memory to numerical decision: How prior probabilities affect cutoff location. *Memory & Cognition*, 5(1), 3–9. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03209184. Crossref: verified. [title] Compares the effect of prior probabilities on cutoff location in recognition memory with a numerical decision task.
- Healy, A. F., & Kubovy, M. (1978). The effects of payoffs and prior probabilities on indices of performance and cutoff location in recognition memory. *Memory & Cognition*, 6(5), 544–553. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03198243. Crossref: verified. [abs-web] In recognition memory the cutoff moved by similar amounts under payoff and prior-probability manipulations, and d′ was unaffected by priors. So this study shows no base-rate vs payoff asymmetry, unlike the categorization work in C.
- Healy, A. F., & Kubovy, M. (1981). Probability matching and the formation of conservative decision rules in a numerical analog of signal detection. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Learning and Memory*, 7(5), 344–354. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.7.5.344. Crossref: verified. [title] Links conservative cutoffs to probability matching in a numerical SDT analogue.
- Ackermann, J. F., & Landy, M. S. (2015). Suboptimal decision criteria are predicted by subjectively weighted probabilities and rewards. *Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics*, 77(2), 638–658. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-014-0779-z. Crossref: verified. [abs-web] With target priors and rewards varied in a visual detection task, criteria were not close to optimal. They were better predicted by the slope of a subjective-utility function than by expected gain.
- Stüttgen, M. C., Yildiz, A., & Güntürkün, O. (2011). Adaptive criterion setting in perceptual decision making. *Journal of the Experimental Analysis of Behavior*, 96(2), 155–176. https://doi.org/10.1901/jeab.2011.96-155. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Pigeons given unequal reinforcement first over-shift toward the favoured response, then drift back toward unbiased responding. Modelled as criterion learning.
- Wickens, C. D., Hollands, J. G., Banbury, S., & Parasuraman, R. (2015). *Engineering psychology and human performance* (4th ed.). Psychology Press. https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315665177. Crossref: verified as a book. **Not verified: whether this edition contains the "sluggish beta" passage.** I didn't read the book. A 2020 preprint (arXiv 2010.00828, found by web search) describes "sluggish beta" as cutoffs insufficiently adjusted to signal probability and payoffs, but I couldn't see whom it cites for the term.

## C. Base-rate versus payoff asymmetry; flat maxima; competition between reward and accuracy (COBRA)

- von Winterfeldt, D., & Edwards, W. (1982). Costs and payoffs in perceptual research. *Psychological Bulletin*, 91(3), 609–622. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.91.3.609. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Argues that payoffs are a weak way to tell subjects what to do. It is widely cited for the flat-maxima argument: near the optimum, expected payoff changes little with the criterion, so incentives to shift are small. The abstract doesn't state that argument, and I didn't read the body.
- Busemeyer, J. R., & Myung, I. J. (1992). An adaptive approach to human decision making: Learning theory, decision theory, and human performance. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 121(2), 177–194. https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.121.2.177. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Rule-competition model with hill-climbing adjustment of the criterion. My inference, not stated in the abstract: a hill-climber on a flat payoff surface learns the criterion slowly and incompletely.
- Maddox, W. T. (1995). Base-rate effects in multidimensional perceptual categorization. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 21(2), 288–301. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.21.2.288. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Observers were sensitive to unequal base rates from the first session, with little conservatism. Those who weren't optimal tended to over-estimate the base-rate difference.
- Maddox, W. T., & Bohil, C. J. (1998). Base-rate and payoff effects in multidimensional perceptual categorization. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 24(6), 1459–1482. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.24.6.1459. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Conservative cutoffs when payoffs were unequal, but extreme (over-shifted) cutoffs when base rates were unequal (Exp. 1). This is the canonical base-rate vs payoff asymmetry. The COBRA label is used in Maddox & Dodd (2001) and Maddox (2002); the abstract I read here doesn't name it.
- Maddox, W. T., & Bohil, C. J. (1998). Overestimation of base-rate differences in complex perceptual categories. *Perception & Psychophysics*, 60(4), 575–592. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03206047. Crossref: verified. [title] Base-rate differences are over-estimated with complex categories.
- Bohil, C. J., & Maddox, W. T. (2001). Category discriminability, base-rate, and payoff effects in perceptual categorization. *Perception & Psychophysics*, 63(2), 361–376. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03194476. Crossref: verified. [abs-web] Conservative cutoff placement in base-rate, payoff and combined conditions, closer to optimal for base rates than payoffs, and closer to optimal when the reward function is steeper (flat-maxima support).
- Maddox, W. T., & Dodd, J. L. (2001). On the relation between base-rate and cost-benefit learning in simulated medical diagnosis. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 27(6), 1367–1384. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.27.6.1367. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] In a simulated diagnosis task, criteria were closer to optimal under base-rate than cost-benefit manipulations, and closer for intermediate discriminability (d′ = 2.2 vs 1.0 or 3.2). Supports the flat-maxima and COBRA hypotheses.
- Maddox, W. T. (2002). Toward a unified theory of decision criterion learning in perceptual categorization. *Journal of the Experimental Analysis of Behavior*, 78(3), 567–595. https://doi.org/10.1901/jeab.2002.78-567. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Review: with unequal base rates the human criterion is nearly optimal; with unequal payoffs it is suboptimal, even when the optimal criterion is identical. Proposes a unified theory (COBRA plus flat maxima). This is the closest human analogue to the pilot's C3 (payoff ρ below base-rate ρ).
- Maddox, W. T., & Bohil, C. J. (2003). A theoretical framework for understanding the effects of simultaneous base-rate and payoff manipulations on decision criterion learning in perceptual categorization. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 29(2), 307–320. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.29.2.307. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Simultaneous base-rate and payoff conditions came out closer to optimal than an independence model predicts. A hybrid flat-maxima + COBRA model fits best.
- Maddox, W. T., Bohil, C. J., & Dodd, J. L. (2003). Linear transformations of the payoff matrix and decision criterion learning in perceptual categorization. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 29(6), 1174–1193. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.29.6.1174. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Criterion learning was best in base-rate conditions, worst when losses followed errors, and intermediate with no losses. Observers were sensitive to payoff-matrix multiplication, as flat maxima predicts.
- Bohil, C. J., & Maddox, W. T. (2003). On the generality of optimal versus objective classifier feedback effects on decision criterion learning in perceptual categorization. *Memory & Cognition*, 31(2), 181–198. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03194378. Crossref: verified. [title] Feedback type (optimal-classifier vs objective) affects criterion learning.
- Maddox, W. T., & Bohil, C. J. (2004). Probability matching, accuracy maximization, and a test of the optimal classifier's independence assumption in perceptual categorization. *Perception & Psychophysics*, 66(1), 104–118. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03194865. Crossref: verified. [title] Probability-matching versus accuracy-maximizing accounts of criterion placement.
- Maddox, W. T., & Bohil, C. J. (2005). Optimal classifier feedback improves cost-benefit but not base-rate decision criterion learning in perceptual categorization. *Memory & Cognition*, 33(2), 303–319. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03195319. Crossref: verified. [title] Per its title, optimal-classifier feedback helps payoff (cost-benefit) learning but not base-rate learning.
- Koß, C., de la Cuesta-Ferrer, L., Stüttgen, M. C., & Jäkel, F. (2025). Reconciling signal-detection models of criterion learning with the generalized matching law. *Computational Brain & Behavior*, 8(1), 111–133. https://doi.org/10.1007/s42113-024-00212-w. Crossref: verified (online 2024, issue 2025). [abs-Crossref] Relates criterion-learning models in SDT (how reinforcement moves the criterion) to the generalized matching law for response allocation.
- Aujla, H. (2022). d′o: Sensitivity at the optimal criterion location. *Behavior Research Methods*, 55(5), 2532–2558. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-022-01913-5. Crossref: verified (online 2022, issue 2023). [abs-web] Proposes a sensitivity index evaluated at the optimal criterion that stays stable when response bias is introduced. Possibly useful as a companion to ρ.
- Erev, I. (1998). Signal detection by human observers: A cutoff reinforcement learning model of categorization decisions under uncertainty. *Psychological Review*, 105(2), 280–298. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.105.2.280. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Replacing the ideal-observer cutoff rule with cutoff reinforcement learning reproduces 19 regularities that SDT can't predict.

## D. Probability matching and criterion-setting accounts

- Thomas, E. A., & Legge, D. (1970). Probability matching as a basis for detection and recognition decisions. *Psychological Review*, 77(1), 65–72. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0028579. Crossref: verified. [title] Probability-matching account of criterion placement.
- Treisman, M., & Williams, T. C. (1984). A theory of criterion setting with an application to sequential dependencies. *Psychological Review*, 91(1), 68–111. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.91.1.68. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] The criterion is set by a long-term process and adjusted by short-term processes that track expected signal probabilities and maximize detections, which explains sequential effects.
- Balakrishnan, J. D. (1999). Decision processes in discrimination: Fundamental misrepresentations of signal detection theory. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance*, 25(5), 1189–1206. https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.25.5.1189. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Distribution-free tests suggest base-rate and payoff manipulations changed the encoding distributions, not the decision rule. This is an alternative reading of "criterion shifts" that matters when interpreting ρ.
- Mueller, S. T., & Weidemann, C. T. (2008). Decision noise: An explanation for observed violations of signal detection theory. *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 15(3), 465–494. https://doi.org/10.3758/PBR.15.3.465. Crossref: verified. [title] Criterion/decision noise accounts for observed SDT violations.
- Benjamin, A. S., Diaz, M., & Wee, S. (2009). Signal detection with criterion noise: Applications to recognition memory. *Psychological Review*, 116(1), 84–115. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0014351. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Noisy-criterion SDT (ND-TSD): criterion variability lowers measured sensitivity and changes the isosensitivity function.

## E. Recognition memory, prevalence, and stated vs experienced base rates

- Estes, W. K., & Maddox, W. T. (1995). Interactions of stimulus attributes, base rates, and feedback in recognition. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 21(5), 1075–1095. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.21.5.1075. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Without feedback, hit and false-alarm rates were independent of the old/new base rate. With feedback, false alarms moved toward the true base rate for some stimuli but not words. A human case of near-zero base-rate use without feedback.
- Rhodes, M. G., & Jacoby, L. L. (2007). On the dynamic nature of response criterion in recognition memory: Effects of base rate, awareness, and feedback. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 33(2), 305–320. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.33.2.305. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Criteria shifted robustly with item-specific study probability, but awareness of the base-rate cue and feedback were key to the shifts.
- Aminoff, E. M., Clewett, D., Freeman, S., Frithsen, A., Tipper, C., Johnson, A., Grafton, S. T., & Miller, M. B. (2012). Individual differences in shifting decision criterion: A recognition memory study. *Memory & Cognition*, 40(7), 1016–1030. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-012-0204-6. Crossref: verified. [title] Large individual differences in how far people shift the criterion with target probability.
- Kantner, J., & Lindsay, D. S. (2012). Response bias in recognition memory as a cognitive trait. *Memory & Cognition*, 40(8), 1163–1177. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-012-0226-0. Crossref: verified. [title] Recognition response bias is stable within people (trait-like), a human analogue of model-specific default strictness.
- Wolfe, J. M., Horowitz, T. S., & Kenner, N. M. (2005). Rare items often missed in visual searches. *Nature*, 435(7041), 439–440. https://doi.org/10.1038/435439a. Crossref: verified. [title] Low target prevalence raises miss rates in visual search.
- Wolfe, J. M., Horowitz, T. S., Van Wert, M. J., Kenner, N. M., Place, S. S., & Kibbi, N. (2007). Low target prevalence is a stubborn source of errors in visual search tasks. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 136(4), 623–638. https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.136.4.623. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] The prevalence effect is a criterion shift, not a sensitivity change. It resists several interventions and is fixed only by brief high-prevalence retraining with feedback. Here humans shift with experienced prevalence, the opposite failure to the pilot's under-shift to a stated rate.
- Wolfe, J. M., & Van Wert, M. J. (2010). Varying target prevalence reveals two dissociable decision criteria in visual search. *Current Biology*, 20(2), 121–124. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2009.11.066. Crossref: verified. [title] Prevalence moves both the target-present criterion and the quitting threshold.
- Levari, D. E., Gilbert, D. T., Wilson, T. D., Sievers, B., Amodio, D. M., & Wheatley, T. (2018). Prevalence-induced concept change in human judgment. *Science*, 360(6396), 1465–1467. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aap8731. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] When a signal becomes rare, people broaden its definition and keep finding it, from colour judgments to ethics. Experienced prevalence moves the concept boundary.
- Swets, J. A., Dawes, R. M., & Monahan, J. (2000). Psychological science can improve diagnostic decisions. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 1(1), 1–26. https://doi.org/10.1111/1529-1006.001. Crossref: verified. [title] Applied review of setting diagnostic thresholds from base rates and costs (SDT in medicine, law, screening).

## F. z-ROC slopes below 1, unequal variance, ROC methods

- Ratcliff, R., Sheu, C., & Gronlund, S. D. (1992). Testing global memory models using ROC curves. *Psychological Review*, 99(3), 518–535. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.99.3.518. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Recognition-memory ROCs imply a new-item familiarity SD about 0.8 of the old-item SD (z-ROC slope ≈ 0.8), independent of strength.
- Ratcliff, R., McKoon, G., & Tindall, M. (1994). Empirical generality of data from recognition memory receiver-operating characteristic functions and implications for the global memory models. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 20(4), 763–785. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.20.4.763. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] z-ROC slope stays constant across strength, list length and category, and varies with word frequency.
- Glanzer, M., Kim, K., Hilford, A., & Adams, J. K. (1999). Slope of the receiver-operating characteristic in recognition memory. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 25(2), 500–513. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.25.2.500. Crossref: verified. [title] Systematic study of recognition z-ROC slopes.
- Van Zandt, T. (2000). ROC curves and confidence judgments in recognition memory. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 26(3), 582–600. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.26.3.582. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Confidence-based z-ROCs changed shape when bias was induced by stimulus probability (Exp. 1) or payoffs (Exp. 2). So confidence isn't scaled directly from familiarity, and ROC shape can depend on the bias manipulation. Directly relevant to C7 (logprob ROCs pooled across conditions).
- Wixted, J. T. (2007). Dual-process theory and signal-detection theory of recognition memory. *Psychological Review*, 114(1), 152–176. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.114.1.152. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Compares the unequal-variance SDT model with the dual-process model; most direct contrasts favour SDT.
- Mickes, L., Wixted, J. T., & Wais, P. E. (2007). A direct test of the unequal-variance signal detection model of recognition memory. *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 14(5), 858–865. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03194112. Crossref: verified. [title] A direct test of the unequal-variance model.
- Yonelinas, A. P., & Parks, C. M. (2007). Receiver operating characteristics (ROCs) in recognition memory: A review. *Psychological Bulletin*, 133(5), 800–832. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.5.800. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Review of ROC methods and models in recognition memory.
- Starns, J. J., Ratcliff, R., & McKoon, G. (2012). Evaluating the unequal-variance and dual-process explanations of zROC slopes with response time data and the diffusion model. *Cognitive Psychology*, 64(1–2), 1–34. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2011.10.002. Crossref: verified. [title] Uses RTs and the diffusion model to adjudicate explanations of z-ROC slopes below 1.
- DeCarlo, L. T. (1998). Signal detection theory and generalized linear models. *Psychological Methods*, 3(2), 186–205. https://doi.org/10.1037/1082-989X.3.2.186. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] SDT models as GLMs (probit and other links). Useful for item-level mixed-effects estimates of d′ and c across conditions.
- Snodgrass, J. G., & Corwin, J. (1988). Pragmatics of measuring recognition memory: Applications to dementia and amnesia. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 117(1), 34–50. https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.117.1.34. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] d′ with c (not β) gives discrimination and bias indices that are independent. Supports reporting c.
- Macmillan, N. A., & Creelman, C. D. (1990). Response bias: Characteristics of detection theory, threshold theory, and "nonparametric" indexes. *Psychological Bulletin*, 107(3), 401–413. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.107.3.401. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Classifies bias indices by assumed distributions and what they measure.
- Stanislaw, H., & Todorov, N. (1999). Calculation of signal detection theory measures. *Behavior Research Methods, Instruments, & Computers*, 31(1), 137–149. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03207704. Crossref: verified. [title] Formulas for d′, c, β and corrections for extreme rates.
- Macmillan, N. A., & Creelman, C. D. (2005). *Detection theory: A user's guide* (2nd ed.). Lawrence Erlbaum. https://doi.org/10.4324/9781410611147. Crossref: verified (Crossref lists the year as 2004 for the digital edition). [abs-OA] Standard textbook.
- Hautus, M. J., Macmillan, N. A., & Creelman, C. D. (2021). *Detection theory: A user's guide* (3rd ed.). Routledge. https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003203636. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Current edition of the same textbook.
- Wickens, T. D. (2002). *Elementary signal detection theory*. Oxford University Press. https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780195092509.001.0001. Crossref: verified (Crossref lists 2001 for the online edition). [abs-OA] Textbook covering equal- and unequal-variance models and ROC estimation.

## G. Base rates and Bayesian conservatism in probability judgment (non-SDT context)

- Phillips, L. D., & Edwards, W. (1966). Conservatism in a simple probability inference task. *Journal of Experimental Psychology*, 72(3), 346–354. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0023653. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Posterior revisions were smaller than Bayes' theorem prescribes (conservatism). This was unaffected by priors, and payoffs improved learning.
- Edwards, W. (1982). Conservatism in human information processing. In D. Kahneman, P. Slovic, & A. Tversky (Eds.), *Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases* (pp. 359–369). Cambridge University Press. https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511809477.026. Crossref: verified (reprint). [abs-OA] Opinion change is orderly and roughly proportional to Bayes, but insufficient in amount. Original: Edwards (1968), in B. Kleinmuntz (Ed.), *Formal representation of human judgment*, Wiley. **The 1968 original isn't in Crossref; not verified.**
- Peterson, C. R., & Beach, L. R. (1967). Man as an intuitive statistician. *Psychological Bulletin*, 68(1), 29–46. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0024722. Crossref: verified. [title] Classic review that people are roughly but conservatively Bayesian.
- Kahneman, D., & Tversky, A. (1973). On the psychology of prediction. *Psychological Review*, 80(4), 237–251. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0034747. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Predictions by representativeness are insensitive to prior probability (base-rate neglect, e.g. lawyer/engineer).
- Bar-Hillel, M. (1980). The base-rate fallacy in probability judgments. *Acta Psychologica*, 44(3), 211–233. https://doi.org/10.1016/0001-6918(80)90046-3. Crossref: verified. [title] Base rates are neglected when judged less relevant than specific evidence.
- Gigerenzer, G., & Hoffrage, U. (1995). How to improve Bayesian reasoning without instruction: Frequency formats. *Psychological Review*, 102(4), 684–704. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.102.4.684. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Frequency formats make Bayesian computation simpler and improve base-rate use. A possible manipulation for the critic study (state the base rate as "18 of 20 solutions").
- Pennycook, G., & Thompson, V. A. (2012). Reasoning with base rates is routine, relatively effortless, and context dependent. *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 19(3), 528–534. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-012-0249-3. Crossref: verified. [title] Per its title, base-rate use in judgment is routine and context-dependent, not effortful.
- Stengård, E., Juslin, P., Hahn, U., & van den Berg, R. (2022). On the generality and cognitive basis of base-rate neglect. *Cognition*, 226, 105160. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2022.105160. Crossref: verified by bibliographic query (preprint 10.1101/2021.03.11.434913 also found). [abs-web; preprint abstract via Crossref] Tests base-rate neglect outside the usual extreme-base-rate corner of problem space. Individual differences are bimodal: some participants almost ignore the base rate, others almost fully use it. An average ρ can hide such a mixture, which matters for item-level analyses of critics.
- Koehler, J. J. (1996). The base rate fallacy reconsidered: Descriptive, normative, and methodological challenges. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 19(1), 1–17. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X00041157. Crossref: verified. [abs-OA] Base rates are almost always used to some degree. How much depends on task structure and representation, and "neglect" is overstated.
